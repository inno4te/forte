Template 01

Cocoa Beans → Powder, Butter & Chocolate

+

Applies directly in Cameroon, Ghana, Nigeria, DRC. Raw beans sell at ~$4,400/tonne; the same tonne as finished chocolate is worth $25,000–35,000+. You don't need a factory to capture a meaningful slice of that gap — a compact grinder can turn farm-gate beans into powder and butter within your first season.

The transformation process
  1. Source & ferment: Buy fully-fermented, sun-dried beans directly from a cooperative — fermentation quality (5–7 days in wooden boxes) determines flavor and cannot be fixed later.
  2. Clean & roast: Sort out debris and broken beans, then roast at 120–150°C for 20–40 minutes depending on bean size, until the shell cracks easily.
  3. Winnow: Crack the roasted beans and separate the shell from the nib using a winnower (manual or motorized fan-based).
  4. Grind: Pass nibs through a melangeur or stone grinder — first producing cocoa liquor/mass, which separates into cocoa solids and cocoa butter under a hydraulic press.
  5. Package the intermediate products: Cocoa powder (from pressed solids) and cocoa butter sell readily to bakeries, cosmetics makers, and confectioners even before you attempt finished chocolate bars.
  6. Optional — conch & temper for bars: Once cash flow is established, add a conche (refines texture over hours) and tempering machine to produce retail chocolate bars.

Template 02

Cassava → Flour, Garri, Starch & Pasta

+

Applies directly in Nigeria, Ghana, DRC, Cameroon. Cassava roots spoil within 48 hours of harvest — the entire economics of this business is about buying at the harvest-glut price and converting to a shelf-stable product before rot sets in.

The transformation process (flour route)
  1. Harvest & peel fast: Process within 24–48 hours of harvest — peel by hand or with a mechanical peeler to reduce cyanogenic compounds and prevent spoilage.
  2. Wash & grate: Wash thoroughly, then grate into a mash using a motorized grater (the single highest-leverage piece of equipment for scale).
  3. Press: Load the mash into sacks and use a screw or hydraulic press to remove excess liquid — this step also removes cyanide-carrying compounds.
  4. Dry: Sun-dry on raised mats or use a solar/mechanical dryer to bring moisture below 12% — this is the step that determines shelf life.
  5. Mill: Pass dried cassava chips through a hammer mill to produce High Quality Cassava Flour (HQCF).
  6. For garri instead: Fry the pressed, fermented mash in a large pan over heat, stirring continuously, instead of sun-drying and milling.
  7. Advanced — pasta/spaghetti: Blend HQCF (typically 20–40%) with wheat flour and an extruder to produce composite-flour pasta — a genuine import-substitution product, though pure-cassava pasta requires specialized gluten-free extrusion equipment.

Template 03

Green Coffee → Roasted, Branded Beans

+

Applies directly in Ethiopia, Kenya, Cameroon. Green beans are commodity-priced; roasted, branded coffee sells for multiples more. This is one of the most accessible transformations because a real specialty market — both domestic café culture and export buyers — already exists and pays a premium for traceable origin.

The transformation process
  1. Source graded green beans: Buy directly from a cooperative or the national commodity exchange, specifying grade and altitude — this is your single biggest quality lever.
  2. Sample roast & cup: Before committing to a batch, roast small samples and taste ("cup") them to understand the flavor profile you're working with.
  3. Roast: Use a drum roaster, tracking time and temperature carefully (typically 8–15 minutes to first crack, longer for darker roasts) — consistency is what buyers pay for.
  4. Rest & degas: Let roasted beans rest 12–48 hours to release CO2 before sealing — packaging too early causes bags to bloat and stales the coffee.
  5. Grade & package: Sort for defects, package in one-way valve bags (lets CO2 out, keeps oxygen out), and label with origin, roast date and roast level.
  6. Get Q-graded (optional but valuable): Formal cupping certification lets you sell into the specialty export market at meaningfully higher prices.

Template 04

Raw Cotton → Yarn, Fabric & Garments

+

Applies directly in Cameroon, Egypt, Nigeria. This is a longer, more capital-intensive chain than most on this list — realistic entry points are at the smaller finishing/garment end rather than industrial spinning, which needs serious capital.

The transformation process (garment-finishing entry point)
  1. Buy finished or semi-finished fabric locally: Rather than starting at raw lint, the realistic small-business entry point is sourcing locally-milled fabric (or importing bolt fabric short-term) and focusing on cut-and-sew finishing.
  2. Design & pattern: Develop a small, focused product line (school uniforms, workwear, or a specific garment category) with clear, repeatable patterns.
  3. Cut: Use pattern-cutting tools to cut fabric in batches, minimizing waste through efficient layout ("marker making").
  4. Sew: Assemble garments on industrial sewing machines — start with a small team (3–6 machines) rather than a full factory line.
  5. Finish & quality-check: Trim threads, press, inspect for defects, and attach labels/branding.
  6. Sell direct or B2B: Target institutional buyers (schools, uniforms, corporate workwear) for predictable bulk orders before pursuing retail.

Template 05

Palm Fruit → Refined Cooking Oil

+

Applies directly in Nigeria, Cameroon, Ghana, DRC. Countries with the palm belt still import large volumes of refined oil due to inefficient local milling — closing this gap doesn't require exporting anything, just supplying the domestic market better than imports do.

The transformation process
  1. Harvest & sterilize: Fresh fruit bunches must be processed within 24–48 hours; sterilize with steam or boiling water to halt enzyme activity that degrades oil quality.
  2. Strip & digest: Separate fruit from bunches (stripping), then mash the fruit in a digester to loosen the oil-bearing pulp from the nut.
  3. Press: Use a screw press to extract crude palm oil from the digested mash.
  4. Clarify: Heat the crude oil and let it settle (or centrifuge) to separate oil from water and sediment.
  5. Refine (for cooking-grade oil): Degum, bleach and deodorize using a small refining unit to remove free fatty acids and improve shelf stability and taste — this step is what separates "crude" from supermarket-shelf oil.
  6. Bottle & brand: Package in retail-size bottles with clear labeling — a genuinely branded local product competes directly with imported oil on price and freshness.

Template 06

Raw Cashew → Shelled, Roasted Nuts

+

Applies directly in Nigeria, Ghana and across West Africa. West Africa grows roughly half the world's raw cashews but historically shipped most of it to Vietnam and India for shelling — the highest-margin, most labor-intensive step, done far from where the nuts are grown.

The transformation process
  1. Dry raw nuts: Sun-dry harvested cashew nuts to below 10% moisture for safe storage and processing.
  2. Steam or roast the shell: Heat treatment (steaming or roasting) softens the shell and neutralizes the caustic cashew shell liquid (CNSL) that can burn skin.
  3. Shell/crack: Use a manual or motorized cracking machine to split shells and extract the kernel — this is the step that requires the most labor and the most care to avoid breaking kernels (whole kernels sell for more).
  4. Peel (testa removal): Remove the thin skin covering the kernel, typically by hand after a light drying/humidifying cycle.
  5. Grade: Sort kernels by size and wholeness (whole white kernels command the highest price; broken/split kernels sell lower).
  6. Roast & season (optional): For retail snack-nut sales, roast and season (salt, spices) before packaging.
  7. Package: Vacuum-seal or use nitrogen-flushed bags to prevent rancidity — cashews are high in oil and spoil faster than most nuts if poorly packaged.

Template 07

Shea Nuts → Cosmetic-Grade Butter

+

Applies directly in Ghana, Nigeria and across the West African "shea belt." This is one of the most proven women-led cooperative business models on the continent — global cosmetics brands pay a real premium for refined, cosmetic-grade butter over crude export-grade.

The transformation process
  1. Collect & boil nuts: Gather fallen shea fruit, remove the pulp, and boil the nuts to prevent germination and ease de-husking.
  2. Dry & crack: Sun-dry the boiled nuts, then crack the shells to extract the kernel.
  3. Roast & grind: Roast kernels to develop flavor/aroma compounds, then grind into a paste using a mill (motorized nut mills dramatically cut labor time versus hand-grinding).
  4. Knead & separate: Knead the paste with water in large basins, working out the oil — this traditional hand-kneading step is being increasingly replaced by mechanical kneaders for scale.
  5. Boil & skim: Boil the extracted oil to purify it, skimming off impurities, then let it cool and solidify into raw shea butter.
  6. Refine for cosmetic grade: Filter and lightly process to remove odor and improve texture/color consistency — this is the step that unlocks cosmetics-brand contracts over crude local-market sales.
  7. Package for target market: Bulk-pack for B2B cosmetics buyers, or retail-package (jars, tubs) for direct-to-consumer sale.

Template 08

Raw Latex → Basic Rubber Goods

+

Applies directly in Cameroon, Nigeria and rubber-growing regions of West/Central Africa. Rubber typically leaves as raw latex or block rubber — the realistic small-business entry point is basic molded-rubber goods (mats, seals, simple industrial parts) rather than competing with large tire manufacturers.

The transformation process
  1. Coagulate the latex: Add a coagulant (formic or acetic acid) to fresh liquid latex to solidify it into sheets.
  2. Press into sheets: Roll the coagulated rubber through a mangle/roller to form thin sheets, squeezing out excess water (ribbed smoked sheet, or RSS, is the traditional format).
  3. Smoke or air-dry: Traditionally smoke-dried in a smokehouse for preservation and color; air-drying works for goods that will be re-melted anyway.
  4. Compound: Mix dried rubber with additives (fillers, curing agents) appropriate to the end product — this determines flexibility, durability and cost.
  5. Mold: Use a heated compression mold to shape the compounded rubber into the target product (mats, gaskets, simple industrial seals).
  6. Cure & finish: Vulcanize under heat and pressure to set the final properties, then trim flash (excess material) and inspect.

Template 09

Groundnuts → Peanut Butter & Protein Flour

+

Applies directly in Nigeria, Senegal and across the Sahel. Low equipment cost relative to most items on this list, with direct usefulness for both local nutrition programs and packaged-food retail — a genuinely dual-purpose business.

The transformation process
  1. Dry & sort: Sun-dry raw groundnuts, then hand-sort to remove damaged or moldy nuts (critical — aflatoxin contamination is the single biggest food-safety risk in this business).
  2. Shell: Use a manual or motorized sheller to remove the outer shell.
  3. Roast: Roast shelled nuts in a rotating drum roaster until golden — even roasting is essential for flavor and safety.
  4. Cool & skin: Cool the roasted nuts, then rub or blanch to remove the thin red skin.
  5. Grind: Use a colloid mill or stone grinder to produce smooth or crunchy peanut butter, adding salt/sugar per your recipe.
  6. For protein flour instead: Press roasted nuts to extract oil first, then grind the defatted cake into a high-protein flour — useful for nutrition programs and baking.
  7. Package: Jar peanut butter with proper sealing; bag protein flour with moisture-barrier packaging.

Template 10

Maize → Flour, Grits & Composite-Flour Pasta

+

Applies directly in Nigeria, Zambia, Kenya, Ethiopia, South Africa. Grown everywhere at massive scale but rarely milled to export or premium-retail quality locally — this template also covers the path toward maize-based pasta/spaghetti, a genuine import-substitution stretch goal.

The transformation process
  1. Clean & dry: Sort out debris and damaged kernels; dry to safe storage moisture (below 13%) to prevent mold.
  2. Dehull (for refined flour): Remove the bran and germ using a dehulling machine if producing refined white flour; skip this step for whole-grain meal.
  3. Mill: Pass through a hammer or roller mill to produce flour or grits at your target grind size.
  4. Sift & grade: Sieve to separate flour from coarser grits/bran, producing multiple product grades from one batch.
  5. Fortify (optional, high-value): Blend in vitamins/minerals (common in nutrition-program supply contracts) using a simple dosing/mixing step.
  6. Pasta/spaghetti route: Blend maize flour with wheat flour (typically 20–30% maize) and semolina, then process through a pasta extruder — pure-maize pasta needs specialized gluten-free extrusion, so composite flour is the realistic first product.
  7. Package: Moisture-barrier bags for flour/grits; standard pasta packaging for the extruded product.

Template 11

Fish & Poultry Farming (Import Substitution)

+

Applies across every country in this report — nearly all of them import frozen fish and poultry despite having the water, feed-crop and climate resources to raise both locally. This is the lowest-capital entry point on the entire list.

The transformation process (catfish pond route)
  1. Train first: Attend a government fisheries-extension or NGO aquaculture course before digging a pond — poor water management is the most common cause of first-year losses.
  2. Build one pond: Start with a single ~200m² earthen or liner pond, sized to your water access and land.
  3. Stock fingerlings: Buy healthy juveniles from a certified hatchery (catfish tolerate lower oxygen conditions than tilapia and are more forgiving for a first attempt).
  4. Feed & monitor: Feed is 60–70% of running cost — secure a reliable local feed miller or supplement with a maize/soy plot to cut input costs.
  5. Manage water quality: Monitor oxygen and ammonia levels; partial water changes prevent the die-offs that end most first attempts.
  6. Harvest at maturity: Typically 5–8 months to harvest weight (800–1,000kg from a 200m² pond).
  7. Pre-arrange buyers: Line up a market vendor, restaurant, or cooperative before harvest so a season's investment isn't sold at a panic discount.
  8. Add poultry (parallel track): Broiler chickens reach market weight in just 6–8 weeks and can run alongside fish farming on the same plot, diversifying cash flow.

Template 12

Rough Timber → Furniture & Veneer

+

Applies directly in Cameroon, DRC, Ghana. Roughly 80%+ of timber leaves as sawn wood or raw logs; furniture and veneer products carry several times the value per cubic metre of rough-sawn export timber.

The transformation process
  1. Source legally-sawn lumber: Buy from a licensed sawmill with proper permits — this protects your business and often your access to better wood grades.
  2. Kiln or air-dry: Wood must be dried to 8–12% moisture before furniture-grade use; air-drying takes weeks to months, a small kiln cuts this dramatically.
  3. Design & template: Develop a focused product line (a few chair/table/cabinet designs) with reusable templates/jigs for consistency.
  4. Cut & shape: Use table saws, planers and routers to cut pieces to precise dimensions and profile edges.
  5. Join & assemble: Construct using appropriate joinery (screws, dowels, mortise-and-tenon) depending on product durability requirements.
  6. Sand & finish: Progressive sanding followed by stain/varnish/oil finishing — finish quality is often what separates a premium-priced piece from a commodity one.
  7. For veneer instead: Slice thin sheets from select logs using a veneer slicer, then press onto substrate boards — a different, higher-throughput product line for architectural/furniture-panel markets.

Template 13

Copper Cathode/Scrap → Wire & Cable

+

Applies directly in Zambia, DRC. Larger-capital template than most on this list, but the Copperbelt's existing cathode supply means the raw-material sourcing problem most manufacturing businesses face is already solved locally.

The transformation process
  1. Source copper cathode or clean scrap: Buy refined cathode locally, or clean/sort copper scrap if starting smaller — purity determines your product's electrical grade.
  2. Melt & cast rod: Melt cathode in an induction or fuel furnace and continuously cast into wire rod using a rod-casting line.
  3. Draw wire: Pass the rod through a series of progressively smaller dies on a wire-drawing machine to reach target gauge.
  4. Anneal: Heat-treat the drawn wire to restore flexibility lost during drawing (cold-worked copper becomes brittle without this step).
  5. Stranding (for cable): Twist multiple fine wires together on a stranding machine to produce flexible cable cores.
  6. Insulate & jacket: Extrude PVC or other insulation over the conductor, then an outer jacket if producing finished electrical cable.
  7. Test & certify: Electrical continuity and insulation-resistance testing before sale — certification opens the door to construction and utility contracts.

Template 14

Rough Gemstones → Cut & Polished

+

Applies directly in Zambia (emeralds), South Africa (diamonds), and gold/gemstone-producing regions generally. Cutting and polishing captures a genuinely large share of a gemstone's final value — this is a skills-first business more than an equipment-first one.

The transformation process
  1. Learn gemology fundamentals first: Understanding stone grading, inclusions and cutting angles is the actual skill being sold here — invest in training before equipment.
  2. Source rough stones: Buy legally-certified rough from licensed dealers or mining cooperatives — traceable, conflict-free sourcing is increasingly required by international buyers.
  3. Plan the cut: Examine the rough stone under magnification to plan cuts that maximize value while working around inclusions.
  4. Preform/shape: Use a trim saw to remove waste material and rough-shape the stone close to its final form.
  5. Facet or cabochon cut: Use a faceting machine (for transparent stones like emerald) or a cabbing machine (for opaque/cabochon stones) to cut precise facets or smooth domed surfaces.
  6. Polish: Progressive polishing with finer abrasives brings out the stone's full brilliance and color.
  7. Grade & certify: Independent grading/certification significantly increases buyer confidence and price, especially for export sales.

Sesame Seed → Oil & Tahini

+

Applies directly in Ethiopia, Nigeria and across the Sahel. Ethiopia in particular exports large volumes of raw sesame to Asian and Middle Eastern buyers who then press it into higher-value tahini and sesame oil — sold back into the same regional markets.

The transformation process
  1. Clean & sort: Remove debris, stones and damaged seeds — sesame's small size makes mechanical cleaning far faster than hand-sorting at any real volume.
  2. Dehull (optional): Remove the outer hull for lighter-colored, milder-tasting oil and tahini; hulled sesame commands a price premium.
  3. Roast: Light roasting develops the characteristic nutty flavor — roast time and temperature directly determine final flavor profile.
  4. Press for oil: Use a screw press (cold-press for premium "virgin" oil, or hot-press for higher yield) to extract sesame oil.
  5. Grind for tahini instead: Grind roasted (hulled or unhulled) seeds in a stone or steel-burr mill until smooth — tahini's texture is entirely a function of grind fineness.
  6. Filter & settle: Let pressed oil settle or filter to remove sediment before bottling.
  7. Package: Bottle oil in dark glass (protects against light degradation); jar tahini with a note to stir before use (natural oil separation is normal).
