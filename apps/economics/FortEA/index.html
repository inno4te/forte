A Political Economy Report — 2026

The wealth leaves raw. The poverty stays refined.

Africa exports its cobalt, copper, oil, cocoa and coffee for cents on the dollar, borrows to survive the shortfall, and buys the finished product back at a markup it can no longer afford. This is not bad luck. It is a designed loop — and it can be redesigned. Ten countries, ten roadmaps, one argument.

The Mechanism

It isn't one bad decision. It's a loop with four gears.

Debt, aid, extraction and governance are usually discussed as separate problems. They are not. Each one feeds the next, and the loop resets every commodity cycle. Understanding the loop is the precondition for breaking it.

01

Raw export, not value chain

Most African economies still sell the first link in a value chain — ore, beans, crude, raw fibre — and let another country do the refining, roasting, smelting or manufacturing that captures the real margin. The continent holds roughly 30% of the world's mineral reserves but a low single-digit share of global manufacturing value-add built on those same minerals.

02

Debt fills the shortfall

When commodity prices dip, or a shock hits, governments borrow to cover the gap — often on non-concessional or opaque terms, sometimes collateralised against future resource revenue. Debt service then competes directly with health, education and infrastructure spending, and the next commodity downturn finds the buffer already gone.

03

Aid and conditionality arrive

Concessional lending and technical support from the IMF, World Bank and bilateral donors are frequently necessary and sometimes stabilising — but conditionality has repeatedly prioritised fiscal austerity and export-led adjustment over the industrial and human-capital investment that would let a country outgrow the next crisis.

04

Governance absorbs the shock

Where institutions are personalised rather than accountable, resource rents and borrowed funds are the easiest thing to capture. Weak procurement oversight, opaque state-owned enterprises and long incumbencies convert a structural economic problem into a political one — and make the first three gears harder to fix.

05

The next generation inherits the gap

With value-added capacity still undeveloped, debt service still consuming the budget, and governance still unreformed, the terms of the next commodity cycle are set before the next government even takes office. Youth unemployment and outward migration are the visible symptom of an invisible structure.

06

The loop resets

A new commodity boom — lithium and cobalt today, as oil and copper before it — creates the same temptation to export raw and borrow against the promise, without using the windfall to build the missing gear: domestic processing, transparent institutions, and leadership that treats public office as stewardship.

Raw export→ Revenue shock→ Debt / Aid→ Conditionality→ Weak institutions absorb it→ Loop resets

Central Africa

🇨🇲 Cameroon Oil · Cocoa · Timber
Debt ~40% GDPInterest 10.6%
+

The mechanism

Cameroon has run IMF-supported programs (ECF/EFF, plus a Resilience and Sustainability Facility) continuously since 2021, yet oil royalties routed through the state hydrocarbons company (SNH) have repeatedly come in below program projections, and non-oil revenue collection keeps missing its own targets. The fiscal gap this leaves is increasingly financed with expensive commercial borrowing — a December 2025 external loan carried a 10.58% effective interest rate, simply to manage a liquidity shortfall.

That liquidity pressure is compounded by two unresolved political costs: a security response to the Anglophone regions' independence movement, and instability following the disputed October 2025 presidential election extending a 43-year incumbency. Both draw budget away from productive investment and toward containment.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Publish full SNH oil-revenue contracts and flows under EITI standards so royalty shortfalls can be audited, not assumed.
  2. Convert the Anglophone crisis into a negotiated devolution settlement — the fiscal bleed from prolonged conflict costs more than most reform packages save.
  3. Build a domestic Treasury-bill and bond market deep enough to end reliance on 10%+ commercial loans for liquidity gaps.
  4. Mandate a rising share of cocoa and timber to be processed domestically before export, phased over a fixed timetable.
  5. Establish an independent electoral and succession framework so leadership transition stops being a fiscal and security risk in itself.

What Cameroon is actually losing

Cocoa: Cameroon is the world's 4th-largest cocoa producer, shipping roughly 180,000–250,000 tonnes of beans a year. It sells most of that crop as raw beans at the farm-gate and export price — not as chocolate. Global development research on cocoa value chains has repeatedly found that producing countries capture somewhere around 6–7% of the final retail value of a chocolate bar; the beans themselves are typically worth less than a tenth of what the finished confectionery sells for once it is roasted, conched, tempered, branded and packaged, almost entirely outside Africa.

Timber: Cameroon holds the second-largest rainforest reserve in Africa — 22.5 million hectares, with 17.5 million hectares commercially exploitable. Yet Volza trade data shows sawn wood and raw pine logs still make up the overwhelming majority of what actually leaves the country (roughly 82% sawn wood, 18% raw log by shipment volume in 2024) rather than furniture, flooring, veneer or engineered panel products, whose finished-goods value runs several multiples higher per cubic metre than rough-sawn export timber.

Oil and gas: Crude petroleum and LNG together were roughly half of 2024 export earnings, yet Cameroon still imports refined fuel products it could in principle produce domestically, and SNH's declining oil production combined with revenue transfers that have repeatedly fallen short of budget projections means the state doesn't even fully capture the value of the barrel it does export before it leaves the ground.

What it takes to build the value chain
01 · Infrastructure
Processing capacity at origin

Grinding and conching facilities near Douala/Yaoundé for cocoa; sawmill-to-furniture and veneer plants in the East and South regions; reliable power and rail/road links from forest and farm belts to port — the physical bottleneck today, not just capital.

02 · Financing
Patient, blended capital

Processing plants need 7–10 year capital, not the short-tenor commercial loans (like the 10.6% December 2025 facility) the state currently relies on. Development-bank concessional lines, blended with private equity in agro-processing, are the realistic route — pure market-rate borrowing makes local processing uncompetitive from day one.

03 · Skills
A trained processing workforce

Chocolate conching, furniture-grade wood finishing and refinery operation require technical and vocational training pipelines Cameroon does not yet run at scale — a direct link to the technical/vocational education gap, not a separate problem.

04 · Policy
Phased export requirements

A published, fixed-timetable rule requiring a rising minimum share of cocoa and timber to be processed domestically before export licensing — predictable enough that investors can plan around it, unlike ad hoc bans that spook capital overnight.

05 · Market access
Branded routes to buyers

Preferential access and branding partnerships (EU cocoa/timber sustainability rules are already reshaping demand) so "Made in Cameroon" chocolate and furniture can reach retail shelves directly, not just anonymous bulk commodity exchanges.

How this changes the narrative: a country that sells $1B of raw cocoa beans is a commodity exporter subject to whatever price the Abidjan and London futures markets set that week. A country that processes even 30–40% of that crop into butter, paste and finished chocolate for export is a manufacturing economy with pricing power, tax base, and skilled jobs that don't disappear when the harvest is poor. The mineral is the same tree. The difference is entirely in what Cameroon chooses to build around it.

🇨🇩 Democratic Republic of Congo Cobalt · Copper
Cobalt share ~70% globalDebt capacity Weak
+

The mechanism

The DRC produces roughly 70% of the world's cobalt and is the second-largest copper producer on earth, yet the IMF classifies its debt-carrying capacity as "weak," with a composite indicator score of 2.33. In February 2025 the government suspended cobalt exports outright to arrest a price collapse caused by global oversupply — a sign of how little pricing power a raw-material exporter holds even as the sole dominant supplier.

Almost none of that cobalt is refined into battery-grade material inside the country. A parallel stream of gold, tin, tungsten and tantalum from artisanal mines in the conflict-affected east is smuggled through neighbouring states to be processed and sold abroad, capturing value the Congolese state never sees. Minerals-for-infrastructure arrangements such as the SICOMINES deal with Chinese partners have swapped resource access for roads and hospitals on terms Congolese civil society has long argued were opaque and imbalanced.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Require a rising, legally binding quota of cobalt to be processed into battery precursor material domestically before export licenses are granted.
  2. Commission an independent, published audit of the SICOMINES resource-for-infrastructure model and renegotiate terms found to be below fair value.
  3. Formalize artisanal and small-scale mining supply chains — bringing the smuggled 10–20% of cobalt output into the legal, taxable economy and improving miner safety.
  4. Redirect security spending freed by the 2025 Rwanda peace framework toward eastern reconstruction and mine-site governance.
  5. Capitalize a sovereign minerals fund from cobalt and copper windfalls, ring-fenced by law from short-term budget raids, to fund health and education directly.

What the DRC is actually losing

Cobalt: a battery-grade EV cell traces back almost entirely to Congolese-mined cobalt, yet the refining into cobalt sulphate — the step that actually feeds battery gigafactories — happens overwhelmingly in China and a handful of other processing hubs. The World Bank's own market analysis of DRC cobalt found the mineral contributed roughly 9% of GDP and 9% of FDI over 2013–18 while providing only about 6.3% of fiscal revenue in 2016 — a gap between what the resource is worth and what the state actually captures from it.

What it takes to build the value chain
01 · Infrastructure
Refining & power

Cobalt sulphate conversion plants require enormous, stable electricity — the single biggest constraint in Katanga/Lualaba's grid today. Grid investment and refinery co-location are inseparable projects, not sequential ones.

02 · Financing
Resource-backed, transparently priced

Future minerals-for-infrastructure deals need published pricing benchmarks and independent valuation — the core complaint against SICOMINES — so financing doesn't quietly become a second extraction channel.

03 · Skills
Metallurgical & safety training

Formalizing artisanal mining requires trained cooperative management and safety supervision at thousands of small sites — a governance and training challenge as large as the engineering one.

04 · Policy
Binding local-processing quotas

A legally fixed, rising minimum share of cobalt required to leave as sulphate rather than ore — enforceable at the export terminal, where compliance is easiest to verify.

05 · Market access
Direct battery-maker contracts

Long-term offtake agreements directly with EV and battery manufacturers (bypassing intermediary traders) so more of the price premium for "responsibly sourced" cobalt reaches Congolese producers.

How this changes the narrative: today the DRC is described, accurately, as "cobalt-rich and dollar-poor" — a country whose mineral wealth shows up in everyone's GDP forecasts except its own citizens' household budgets. A DRC that refines even a quarter of its cobalt into sulphate before export converts a raw-material dependency into an industrial one, with the tax base, skilled employment and pricing leverage that raw ore exports structurally cannot provide.

West Africa

🇳🇬 Nigeria Crude Oil
Oil export share ~90%Tax/GDP Low
+

The mechanism

Africa's largest economy has for decades earned roughly 90% of its export income from crude oil while importing most of its refined fuel — paying a processing markup on its own resource. The 2023 removal of the fuel subsidy and the float of the naira were necessary corrections to years of unsustainable fiscal practice, but they landed as an inflation shock on a population with little cushion, while one of the world's lowest tax-to-GDP ratios has left the state unable to widen its revenue base beyond oil.

Pipeline vandalism and large-scale oil theft further divert revenue that should reach the treasury, and historic opacity at the state oil company has made it difficult to verify how much of the resource wealth is even collected before policy questions of spending begin.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Expand the non-oil tax net — broadening formal-sector and digital-economy taxation rather than repeatedly returning to oil and subsidy levers.
  2. Scale domestic refining capacity so Nigeria stops exporting crude and re-importing refined fuel at a processing markup on its own resource.
  3. Ring-fence subsidy-removal savings into transparent, targeted cash transfers, with public reporting that closes the trust gap left by past leakages.
  4. Back agriculture and fintech/tech export sectors with the same policy priority historically reserved for oil.
  5. Complete state oil company (NNPC) accounting reform and publish reconciled production and remittance figures on a fixed schedule.

What Nigeria is actually losing

The crude-for-refined round trip: for most of the last two decades Nigeria exported crude oil and then imported the majority of its own petrol, diesel and kerosene back — paying international refiners' margins, freight, and insurance twice on the same barrel of oil. Chronically under-maintained state refineries in Port Harcourt, Warri and Kaduna ran at a fraction of capacity for years, so the "processing" step of Nigeria's largest export happened almost entirely offshore.

Oil theft and pipeline losses: tens of thousands of barrels per day have historically been lost to pipeline vandalism, illegal bunkering and theft in the Niger Delta — revenue that never reaches either an export manifest or the treasury, compounding the value already lost to the refining round-trip.

What it takes to build the value chain
01 · Infrastructure
Refining at true scale

The 650,000 bpd Dangote refinery is the first structural move in a generation — the task now is securing enough domestically-sourced crude supply to run it near capacity, plus rehabilitating or replacing the state refineries.

02 · Financing
Private capital, public offtake certainty

Refining and petrochemicals need long-horizon private investment; government's job is guaranteeing predictable domestic crude allocation and fuel-pricing policy so investors aren't exposed to the next subsidy reversal.

03 · Skills
Petrochemical & engineering talent

Nigeria already has a large technical graduate pool; the gap is industry-specific refinery operations and petrochemical-downstream training, plus retaining that talent against outward migration.

04 · Policy
Domestic crude supply obligation

A binding requirement that a rising share of Nigerian-produced crude be sold to domestic refiners at transparent, market-linked pricing, closing the incentive to export everything raw regardless of local refining capacity.

05 · Market access
Regional fuel export hub

A fully-supplied domestic refining sector positions Nigeria to export refined product to West African neighbours instead of importing alongside them — turning a subregional dependency into a subregional market.

How this changes the narrative: a Nigeria that imports the fuel it could refine from its own crude is, functionally, subsidising other countries' refining industries with its own resource. A Nigeria that refines at scale converts the same barrel of oil into fuel-price stability, refining-sector jobs, and a genuine shot at becoming West Africa's supplier rather than its competitor for the same imported product.

🇬🇭 Ghana Gold · Cocoa
2022 Default2025 Stabilizing
+

The mechanism

Ghana defaulted on its external debt in December 2022. Domestic debt was restructured through 2023 and Eurobonds were finally exchanged in October 2024 — nearly two years after the crisis began, a delay that itself carried real economic cost. The underlying vulnerability persists: cocoa and gold still dominate export earnings, cocoa-sector governance under COCOBOD has struggled with weak harvests and financing shortfalls, and unpaid arrears to independent power producers keep re-creating fiscal holes even as headline numbers improve.

Encouragingly, by late 2025 the IMF was reporting single-digit inflation for the first time since 2021, stronger gold and cocoa exports, and a materially improved debt trajectory — evidence that disciplined reform can work, if the discipline outlasts the program.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Legislate the 2025 stabilization gains into a binding fiscal rule that survives beyond the current IMF program's end date.
  2. Raise domestic cocoa processing well above the current sub-30% share — Ghana grows the beans; someone else keeps most of the chocolate margin.
  3. Resolve the energy-sector arrears cycle (IPP debt) permanently rather than repeatedly deferring it into the next fiscal year.
  4. Diversify export earnings into services and the digital economy so two commodities no longer set the country's fiscal weather.
  5. Establish an independent debt management office empowered to block a repeat of the pre-2022 non-concessional borrowing pattern.

What it takes to build the value chain
01 · Infrastructure
Grinding & conching capacity

Ghana already hosts several cocoa-processing plants (including foreign-invested grinding facilities); the gap is scaling conching-and-moulding capacity so finished chocolate, not just intermediate cocoa liquor, is what leaves the port.

02 · Financing
COCOBOD balance-sheet reform

COCOBOD's own borrowing to pre-finance farmer payments has been a recurring fiscal stress point; separating farmer-price guarantees from processing-plant investment financing would stop one from crowding out the other.

03 · Skills
Confectionery & refining technicians

Food-science and refining technical training tied directly to the processing plants being built, so operating capacity doesn't sit idle for lack of trained staff — a common failure mode in first-generation processing investment.

04 · Policy
A rising minimum grind ratio

A published, phased requirement that a growing share of the annual crop be ground domestically before raw-bean export licenses are issued — giving processors certainty of bean supply.

05 · Market access
Direct-to-brand partnerships

Long-term supply agreements with confectionery brands for Ghana-processed cocoa products, capturing origin-branding premiums increasingly demanded by ethically-sourced chocolate markets in Europe and North America.

How this changes the narrative: Ghana's 2022 default is often told as a cautionary tale about borrowing. It is equally a story about a country that grows a fifth of the world's cocoa and still imports much of the chocolate its own citizens eat. Closing that loop — not just restructuring the debt — is what would keep the next commodity downturn from writing the same story again.

North Africa

🇪🇬 Egypt Suez Canal · Gas
Debt ~90% GDPDevaluations 3 since 2016
+

The mechanism

Egypt is one of the IMF's largest borrowers and has devalued its currency three times since 2016. A large military-owned economic footprint competes directly with private firms for contracts and credit, dulling the private-sector-led growth that reform programs are meant to unlock. Billions have gone into prestige infrastructure — most visibly the New Administrative Capital — while Suez Canal revenue, one of the country's most reliable hard-currency earners, proved suddenly fragile when Red Sea shipping attacks cut transit traffic.

The result is a familiar cycle: currency shock, IMF program, Gulf bridge financing, partial reform, and a return to vulnerability the next time an external shock — a war, a shipping-route disruption, a global rate hike — arrives.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Shrink the military and state-owned footprint in commercial sectors so private firms can compete for financing and contracts on equal terms.
  2. Pause new prestige mega-projects and redirect capital toward export-oriented manufacturing and logistics.
  3. Build a dedicated Suez Canal revenue stabilization fund to absorb geopolitical shipping shocks without triggering a fresh currency crisis.
  4. Broaden the tax base beyond consumption taxes that fall hardest on lower-income households.
  5. Convert Gulf financial support from repeated balance-of-payments bailouts into equity investment in productive, revenue-generating assets.

Export-zone manufacturing

Canal-adjacent logistics hub

Use the Canal's unique geography to build a genuine transshipment and light-manufacturing hub, not just a toll point — capturing value from goods in transit, not only their passage fee.

🇹🇳 Tunisia Phosphate · Tourism
IMF deal Rejected 2023Phosphate Collapsed output
+

The mechanism

Tunisia was once among the world's largest phosphate exporters; repeated unrest and underinvestment in the Gafsa mining basin have since collapsed output far below capacity. In 2023, President Kais Saied publicly rejected the conditions attached to a nearly $2 billion IMF loan — including subsidy cuts he called foreign "diktats" — leaving the country to rely on central bank financing and bilateral loans without an anchoring reform program.

The result is a economy stuck between two unattractive paths: externally imposed austerity that risks the same social unrest that triggered the 2011 revolution, or unanchored borrowing that postpones adjustment while eroding investor confidence. Tourism, the other major foreign-currency earner, remains concentrated in a narrow seasonal and geographic band.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Rebuild phosphate output through negotiated agreements with Gafsa basin communities rather than top-down production mandates that have repeatedly triggered shutdowns.
  2. Design a homegrown subsidy-reform path — targeted cash transfers replacing blanket subsidies — that preserves legitimacy while closing the fiscal gap an external program would otherwise force.
  3. Diversify tourism into higher-value and off-season markets rather than competing purely on volume.
  4. Restore judicial independence and rule-of-law guarantees investors cite as a precondition for renewed confidence.

The mechanism

Ethiopia became the first African sovereign to seek debt treatment under the G20 Common Framework in 2021, after the Tigray conflict compounded existing fiscal strain; a Eurobond default followed and a birr devaluation arrived in 2024 as part of an IMF-backed reform.   3. Build domestic coffee roasting and branding capacity so more of the world's coffee margin is captured before the bean ever leaves the country.
  4. Pursue durable political settlement in conflict-affected regions to redirect security spending toward reconstruction.
  5. Create an independent fiscal council with authority to flag unsustainable borrowing before it becomes a crisis, not after.

The mechanism

Zambia became the first African country to default during the COVID-19 era, in November 2020. Copper, which drives 70–75% of export revenue and 13% of GDP, left the economy fully exposed when prices weakened. What followed was a four-year restructuring ordeal under the G20 Common Framework, repeatedly stalled by disputes between Chinese official creditors and Eurobond holders over "comparable treatment" — a structural flaw in the global debt-resolution architecture, not a Zambian policy failure. By late 2025 S&P had upgraded Zambia's rating to CCC+, but the underlying copper dependency, now made worse by climate-driven drought cutting hydropower output, remains unaddressed.

Roadmap beyond the trap

  1. Adopt a transparent, copper-revenue-linked borrowing framework that rules out the kind of opaque collateralized lending that complicated the last restructuring.
  2. Fast-track domestic cathode and copper-wire processing capacity so more of the metal's value is captured before export.
  3. Diversify power generation into solar, given Zambia's resource endowment, to reduce drought-driven hydropower vulnerability.
  4. Push, alongside peer nations, for G20 Common Framework reform so future restructurings take months rather than years.
  5. Reinvest copper windfalls into agriculture — already 20% of GDP and 59% of the labour force — to build resilience beyond mining.

The mechanism

The Economists Disagree On The Fix — Not On The Diagnosis

What the world's leading development economists actually argue.

This is a genuinely contested field — economists who agree the status quo isn't working disagree sharply on why, and on what replaces it. Presenting that real disagreement is more useful than pretending there's consensus where there isn't.

Dambisa Moyo
Zambian economist · former Goldman Sachs & World Bank · author, Dead Aid
"Aid is not benign — it's malignant. It's not a passive process — waiting around, doing nothing. It perpetuates the cycle of corruption, indebtedness."

Moyo's central argument: aid-dependent governments answer to donors instead of citizens, breaking the basic accountability loop that makes democracy self-correcting. She argues for market access, foreign direct investment and municipal bond markets over concessional aid — and has praised China's transactional investment model over Western aid conditionality, a position that remains sharply contested.

Interviewed by NPR & Al Jazeera; "Dead Aid" (2009)

Jeffrey Sachs
Columbia University · former UN Special Advisor · aid-effectiveness advocate
"The debate about foreign aid has become farcical" — yet Sachs maintains that well-targeted aid (bed nets, vaccines, agricultural inputs) has demonstrably saved lives at low cost, and that blanket aid-skepticism ignores this record.

Sachs's rejoinder to Moyo: the argument that aid categorically fails ignores specific, measurable interventions that worked. His deeper point for this report: the debate is more productive when it separates humanitarian and health aid (where evidence of impact is strong) from budget-support and infrastructure lending (where conditionality and governance problems are real and well-documented).

"Aid Ironies," jeffsachs.org

Ha-Joon Chang
Cambridge University · institutional economist
Chang argues Africa already has more individual entrepreneurs per capita than almost any region — and that the proliferation of microcredit-funded petty trading is precisely why so many economies stay trapped rather than industrializing.

His broader thesis, from Kicking Away the Ladder: every currently-rich country used tariffs, subsidies and state-directed industrial policy to build manufacturing before opening to free trade — then advised developing countries to skip that stage entirely. He argues IMF/World Bank orthodoxy has functionally asked Africa to compete globally without ever being allowed to build the protected domestic industry that built everyone else's wealth.

"Kicking Away the Ladder" (2002); The Conversation, 2025

William Easterly
NYU · former World Bank research economist
Easterly's core critique: top-down, technocratically-designed aid programs ("planners") consistently underperform against the incremental, locally-accountable, market-tested efforts of "searchers" who must answer to the people they serve.

His research repeatedly found no statistically robust relationship between aggregate aid volumes and growth outcomes across decades of data — a finding that unsettled the aid-effectiveness field and forced a shift toward more rigorously evaluated, smaller-scale interventions (the same shift that later produced the RCT-driven "randomista" movement in development economics).

"The White Man's Burden" (2006); CGDev commentary

Thomas Piketty
Paris School of Economics · inequality & capital researcher
Piketty's global inequality research situates Africa's debt burden within a wider argument: that returns to capital in wealthy, creditor nations have systematically outpaced growth in debtor economies, mechanically widening the gap regardless of individual-country policy choices.

He has specifically argued for reparative wealth transfers and progressive global taxation — including a global minimum tax on capital — as structural corrections, on the grounds that colonial-era extraction and ongoing capital-return asymmetries cannot be fully undone by domestic reform alone, however well-executed.

"Capital in the Twenty-First Century" (2013); "A Brief History of Equality" (2021)

Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala
WTO Director-General · former Nigerian Finance Minister · former World Bank MD
From inside two finance ministries and the World Bank itself, Okonjo-Iweala has argued that debt transparency and domestic revenue mobilization matter more than the aid-versus-no-aid debate — that the real failure is opaque borrowing terms and weak tax collection, not external assistance per se.

As Nigeria's finance minister she pushed for the "excess crude account" and published budget transparency reforms that were later reversed — a case study, in her own telling, of how fragile technocratic reform is against entrenched interests unless it's locked into law and institutions, not just policy.

Speeches at WTO & Brookings; "Fighting Corruption Is Dangerous" (2018)

Where they actually agree: across this ideological range — from Moyo's aid-skepticism to Piketty's case for global redistribution — every one of these economists agrees the current combination of commodity-export dependence, opaque debt, and weak domestic accountability is not a sustainable equilibrium. They disagree sharply on whether the fix is less aid, better aid, more redistribution, or more industrial policy. This report's case-by-case roadmaps borrow from all four traditions deliberately, because no single school has the complete answer for every country profiled here.

Fish farming (aquaculture) & poultry rearing

Cameroon and much of West and Central Africa import a large share of the fish and poultry consumed domestically — frozen mackerel and chicken parts shipped in from Europe and Asia — despite having the water, feed-crop and climate resources to raise both locally. A catfish or tilapia pond, or a broiler poultry operation, is one of the lowest-capital entry points into import-substitution available to an individual or family.

Starter pond (catfish, 200m²)$800–1,500
Fingerlings (1,000 juveniles)$150–300
Feed, 6-month cycle$400–700
Harvest weight at maturity~800–1,000 kg
Local market price per kg$3–5
Typical cycle net margin30–45%
  • Learn before you build

    Attend a government fisheries-extension or NGO aquaculture training program (many run free short courses) before digging a pond — pond failure from poor water management is the most common first-year loss.

  • Start with one pond, one species

    Catfish tolerate lower water-oxygen conditions than tilapia and are more forgiving for a first attempt; expand to a second pond or species only after one full successful cycle.

  • Secure a feed supply chain

    Feed is 60–70% of running cost — locate a reliable local feed miller or consider growing a maize/soy supplement plot to reduce input costs over time.

  • Line up buyers before harvest

    Pre-arrange sales with a local market vendor, restaurant, or informal cooperative before the fish reach harvest weight, so a six-month investment isn't sold in a panic at a discount.

  • Reinvest into a second cycle

    Use first-cycle profit to add a second pond or scale into poultry, which has an even shorter cycle (6–8 weeks to broiler weight) and can run alongside fish farming on the same plot.

  • Formalize and seek group financing

    Join or form a farmer cooperative to access group microfinance, bulk feed discounts, and shared transport to market — the single biggest lever for moving from subsistence to a genuine small business.

Small-scale cocoa processing (butter, powder, local chocolate)

You do not need an industrial grinding plant to start capturing cocoa value-chain margin. Small artisan processors across Ghana and Cameroon are already turning farm-gate beans into cocoa powder, butter and simple chocolate bars for local and regional markets using compact, mid-cost equipment.

Small roaster/grinder setup$3,000–8,000
Raw beans, 1 tonne~$4,000–4,500
Yield: powder, butter, cake~950 kg saleable
Local/regional sale price2–3× raw bean cost
Realistic first-year scale1–5 tonnes of beans
  • Source beans directly from growers

    Build a relationship with a nearby cocoa cooperative for consistent, quality-graded bean supply — direct sourcing avoids middlemen and secures a stable input price.

  • Master fermentation and drying quality control

    Most quality loss happens before processing even starts; poorly fermented or dried beans cap the price and flavor of everything made afterward.

  • Start with cocoa powder, not chocolate

    Powder and butter require simpler equipment and have reliable regional demand (bakeries, cosmetics, pharma) — treat finished chocolate bars as a phase-two product once cash flow is established.

  • Target regional B2B buyers first

    Bakeries, small confectioners and cosmetics makers are more reliable early customers than retail consumers, who require branding and packaging investment you may not yet have.

  • Pursue food-safety certification early

    Certification opens access to supermarket chains and export markets — the single biggest jump in margin, but it takes months to arrange, so start the paperwork alongside your first production cycle.

Food processing & preservation (tomato paste, dried fruit, cassava flour)

Seasonal gluts of tomatoes, mangoes, cassava and plantains routinely rot at the farm gate for lack of processing or storage — while the same countries import tinned tomato paste and packaged snack foods. Small-scale drying, canning and milling operations convert a perishable glut into a shelf-stable, sellable product.

Solar dryer / small mill setup$500–2,500
Raw produce (seasonal glut price)Often 60–80% below peak
Shelf life, dried/milled product6–18 months
Typical margin vs. raw sale2–4×
  • Buy at the seasonal low, not the daily market

    Time purchasing for harvest-glut periods when farmers are most eager to sell — this is where the biggest margin in the entire business sits, before any processing happens.

  • Choose one product, master it

    Cassava flour, sun-dried tomatoes, or dried mango each require different equipment and food-safety handling — spreading across all three too early dilutes quality and capital.

  • Invest in proper packaging before scaling

    Vacuum-sealed or well-labeled packaging is often the difference between a market-stall product and one that a supermarket or export buyer will consider.

  • Build relationships with school feeding & NGO food programs

    Institutional buyers (school meal programs, humanitarian food procurement) offer large, recurring orders that are more stable than retail sales while a brand is being built.

Digital & AI-enabled services (agri-tech, fintech-adjacent, remote services)

Kenya's fintech sector proves the model in this report's own case studies: value creation that requires no mineral, no import substitution, and almost no physical capital — only connectivity, skill, and market access. AI tools now make this route accessible with a fraction of the training time that fintech required a decade ago.

Laptop + connectivity setup$300–700
AI-assisted skills ramp-up timeWeeks, not years
Addressable marketGlobal, not just local
Typical entry servicesData/AI annotation, agri-advisory bots, bookkeeping
  • Pick a narrow, in-demand skill

    AI-assisted bookkeeping, customer support, content localization or basic data annotation are realistic entry points with fast skill-acquisition curves using freely available AI tools.

  • Build an AI-powered farmer advisory micro-service

    Use accessible AI tools to offer smallholder farmers simple, local-language crop-disease identification or planting-schedule advice via WhatsApp — a genuinely new service category barely possible five years ago.

  • Sell to diaspora and regional clients first

    Diaspora communities and regional businesses are often more willing to pay for reliable digital services from a known cultural context than a fully anonymous global marketplace.

  • Form a digital cooperative

    Pooling a small group's skills (design, writing, bookkeeping, AI-tool fluency) into a shared service cooperative can win larger contracts than any one freelancer could alone.

The Other Half Of The Leak

What Africa buys from abroad that it could grow, make or process at home.

The case studies above focus on what leaves raw. This is the mirror image: everyday goods African countries import in bulk, despite having the raw material, climate or existing partial capacity to produce them domestically. Every item here is a smaller-scale version of the same value-chain argument — and a more immediate one, because it doesn't require a mining concession to start.

Refined vegetable & palm oil

High import bill

Nigeria, Cameroon and Ghana all have significant palm-oil growing regions, yet import substantial volumes of refined cooking oil due to under-invested local milling and refining capacity.

Opportunity: small-to-mid-scale palm oil mills near existing plantations, replacing imported refined oil with domestically milled product.

Poultry & frozen fish

High import bill

Frozen chicken parts and mackerel are among the most commonly imported food items across West and Central Africa, despite domestic poultry and aquaculture capacity existing at small scale already (see the Fish & Poultry pathway above).

Opportunity: the exact same aquaculture and poultry pathway detailed in this report's Business Pathways section.

Refined petroleum products

Very high import bill

Nigeria has historically exported crude and imported the majority of its own refined petrol and diesel — the exact round-trip detailed in this report's Nigeria case study, now beginning to shift with the Dangote refinery.

Opportunity: continued domestic refining capacity build-out across oil-producing states (Cameroon, Angola, Ghana) following Nigeria's example.

Rice

High import bill

West Africa, including Nigeria, Senegal and Cameroon's northern regions, imports large volumes of rice from Asia despite significant domestic growing capacity and government self-sufficiency programs launched repeatedly over the past two decades.

Opportunity: local milling and parboiling facilities that improve the market competitiveness (grain quality, consistent packaging) of domestically grown rice against imports.

Finished textiles & garments

Moderate–high import bill

Cotton-growing countries like Cameroon, Egypt and Tunisia export raw cotton and fabric while importing a significant share of finished clothing — mirroring the cocoa and coffee pattern in a different sector.

Opportunity: garment assembly and finishing capacity co-located with existing textile mills, targeting both domestic and regional (ECOWAS/CEMAC) markets.

Cement & construction inputs

Moderate import bill

Despite abundant limestone deposits across the continent, several countries still import a meaningful share of cement and finished construction materials rather than expanding domestic clinker production.

Opportunity: mid-scale regional cement plants, which Dangote Cement and a handful of others have already proven viable at continental scale.

Packaged & processed foods

Growing import category

Tinned tomato paste, packaged snack foods and bottled juices are increasingly imported even in countries producing the raw tomatoes, fruit and grain those products are made from — a direct match to the Food Processing pathway above.

Opportunity: small-scale canning, bottling and packaging operations targeting the specific gap between local raw abundance and imported processed substitutes.

Pharmaceuticals & basic medical supplies

High import bill

The large majority of medicines consumed across Sub-Saharan Africa are imported, including many basic generics that require no advanced biotechnology to manufacture locally.

Opportunity: generic-drug manufacturing partnerships and regional pooled-procurement manufacturing hubs, an area several African Union initiatives are now actively targeting.

Furniture & finished wood products

Moderate import bill

Countries with major timber reserves like Cameroon and Gabon still import a share of finished furniture and wood products, directly echoing the raw-log export pattern detailed in the Cameroon case study.

Opportunity: furniture and veneer workshops co-located with sawmills, capturing the finishing-stage value currently exported as rough timber.

Beyond Cocoa And Cobalt

The next candidates for transformation — and how, specifically.

The case studies above focus on each country's largest export. But the same "raw in, finished value out" pattern repeats across a much longer list of crops and materials already being grown or produced at scale across the continent. Here is a working list of what qualifies, and the concrete first processing step each one needs.

Cassava

Nigeria (world's #1 producer), DRC, Ghana, Cameroon

Africa grows over half the world's cassava, mostly consumed fresh or as basic fufu/garri with high post-harvest spoilage — cassava roots begin degrading within 48 hours of harvest.

How: village-level flash-drying and milling into high-quality cassava flour (HQCF) — usable in bread, biscuits and as an industrial starch input, with a shelf life of months instead of days.

Maize / corn

Nigeria, Zambia, Kenya, Ethiopia, South Africa

Grown at massive scale for subsistence and animal feed, but rarely milled locally into higher-margin corn flour, starch, or corn-syrup industrial inputs at export quality.

How: mid-scale dry-milling plants producing graded corn flour and grits for both domestic packaged-food markets and regional export, reducing image reliance on imported wheat flour substitutes.

Cotton

Cameroon, Egypt, Mali, Burkina Faso, Benin

Several West and Central African states are major raw cotton exporters — Egypt's long-staple cotton is globally prized — yet most raw cotton is ginned only lightly before export, with spinning and weaving happening elsewhere.

How: regional spinning and weaving mills (several West African states have discussed a joint textile zone) to export finished fabric and garments instead of baled lint.

Shea nuts / shea butter

Ghana, Nigeria, Burkina Faso, Mali

The "shea belt" produces the raw nut behind a global cosmetics ingredient, but much of it is exported as crude, unrefined butter rather than the cosmetic-grade product international beauty brands actually buy at a premium.

How: women-led cooperative refining into cosmetic-grade shea butter — already a proven, replicable small-cooperative model across the shea belt — plus direct contracts with cosmetics brands.

Cashew nuts

Côte d'Ivoire (world's #1 grower), Nigeria, Tanzania

West Africa grows roughly half the world's raw cashews, yet historically processed only a small share domestically — the shelling and roasting stage (where most of the value and jobs sit) has largely happened in Vietnam and India instead.

How: continued build-out of domestic shelling and roasting capacity, a shift several West African governments have already begun actively incentivizing with export levies on raw nuts.

Natural rubber

Cameroon, Nigeria, Côte d'Ivoire, Liberia

Rubber is typically exported as raw latex or basic block rubber, while the higher-value tire, industrial-rubber and glove manufacturing stages happen almost entirely in Asian and Western manufacturing hubs.

How: mid-scale rubber-goods manufacturing (gloves, hoses, basic industrial parts) co-located with plantations, targeting regional construction and healthcare-sector demand first.

Groundnuts (peanuts)

Nigeria, Senegal, Sudan, Chad

Historically one of West Africa's signature export crops, groundnuts are still largely sold raw or minimally processed into basic oil, while peanut butter, protein-flour and snack-food product lines remain underdeveloped locally.

How: small-to-mid-scale peanut butter and protein-flour processing — directly useful for both local nutrition programs and packaged-food export, with low equipment cost relative to other crops on this list.

Palm fruit

Nigeria, Cameroon, DRC, Ghana

West and Central Africa is palm oil's birthplace, yet Southeast Asia now dominates global refined-palm-oil trade — much of Africa's own crop is milled at low efficiency, with real losses at the crude-to-refined stage.

How: modernized, efficient milling and refining capacity near existing plantations — directly closing the "refined vegetable oil" import gap flagged in the Import Substitution section above.

Sesame seed

Ethiopia, Sudan, Nigeria, Tanzania

Ethiopia in particular exports large volumes of raw sesame seed to Asian and Middle Eastern buyers, who then press it into the higher-value tahini and sesame-oil products sold back into regional and global markets.

How: domestic cold-press oil and tahini production, targeting both the fast-growing Middle Eastern export market and the underserved domestic packaged-food sector.

The Missing Gear

No roadmap survives extractive leadership.

Every policy fix above assumes someone in office will actually implement it — past the point where doing so costs them politically. That assumption is the real fault line. The evidence across these ten countries points to one recurring difference between reform that sticks and reform that reverses: whether leadership treats public office as a resource to extract from, or a trust to serve.

The pattern that fails

Extractive Leadership

  • Power is personalised — the state's institutions bend around one office rather than constraining it
  • Incumbency extends indefinitely; succession becomes a national risk event rather than a routine transition
  • Resource rents and borrowed funds flow through opaque, personally-influenced channels
  • Loyalty is rewarded over competence in appointments to oversight and financial institutions
  • Criticism and independent media are treated as threats to be managed, not information to be used
  • Reform is adopted under external pressure and quietly reversed once the pressure lifts
The pattern that compounds

Servant Leadership

  • Power is institutional — checks on the executive hold regardless of who occupies it
  • Term limits are honoured as a feature, not negotiated away as an inconvenience
  • Resource revenue and public borrowing are disclosed on a fixed, auditable schedule
  • Appointments to courts, central banks and audit offices prioritise independence and competence
  • Dissent and a free press are treated as an early-warning system, not a threat
  • Reform outlives the program or the pressure that first demanded it, because it was owned locally

Servant leadership is not a slogan. It's an operating model.

The term comes from Robert Greenleaf's 1970 essay The Servant as Leader: a leader's first test is whether those served grow as people — more autonomous, more capable, more likely to themselves become servants. Nelson Mandela's single term and negotiated reconciliation, Seretse Khama's diamond-revenue stewardship in early Botswana, and Julius Nyerere's public willingness to admit policy failure are imperfect but real African precedents for what this looks like in practice. Instituting it at scale is a systems question, not a character question — it requires structures that make service the path of least resistance for whoever holds office next.

Constrain the office

Enforce existing term limits and judicial independence before writing new laws — most African constitutions already contain the tools; the gap is enforcement.

Decentralize accountability

Push budget authority and audit visibility down to regional and municipal levels, where citizens can see the gap between promise and delivery directly.

Teach it early

Embed leadership-as-service in civic and school curricula, so the expectation is set long before anyone reaches office — not imposed on them once they arrive.

Build the pipeline

Faith communities, professional associations and youth organizations are under-used training grounds for service-oriented leadership — invest in them deliberately.

Reward the exit

Create honoured, well-resourced post-office roles for leaders who step down on schedule, so leaving power is not a leader's greatest personal risk.

The uncomfortable truth this report insists on: the World Bank, the IMF and bilateral donors have real, documented responsibility for conditionality that prioritised austerity over investment, and for financing arrangements that were not always transparent to the citizens who would repay them. That responsibility is named throughout this report and should not be minimised.

But institutional reform in Washington will not arrive on any African country's timetable, and waiting for it has already cost decades. Leadership, governance and value-chain policy are the levers every country on this page can pull today, regardless of what happens at the next Bretton Woods meeting. Both truths matter. Only one is fully within Africa's own hands.
